About the Lab

Porcupine Labs is an independent nonprofit in formation, based in the San Francisco Bay Area and selected into the Catalyze Impact biosecurity accelerator in 2026. We work at the boundary where AI systems direct physical laboratory hardware.

Laboratory automation is decades old and installed at scale. What changed is who writes the script. Synthesis screening, cryptographic provenance and model evaluation each work at the point where they sit: acquisition, the digital artifact, the instruction. The instrument itself is unchecked, and the record it leaves does not distinguish a protocol a person wrote from one a model wrote.

Research Approach

We evaluate properties of execution rather than properties of models. That means testing how agentic planners, laboratory execution software and robotic instrumentation behave under ambiguous or adversarial instructions, and asking whether a facility's own records are enough to establish afterward what was done.

Our first study is read-only. For a completed run chosen after the fact, we ask how much of it a facility can reconstruct from the systems it already runs: the electronic lab notebook, the inventory and sample management system, instrument logs, the scheduler. Those systems were built to operate a laboratory and to satisfy audit, and the open question is whether what they capture is sufficient to establish what an agent-directed run did, down to which software version and which configuration stood behind each decision. What we find turns into technical specification work on log integrity, provenance, and capability-scoped authorization.

Mission

Biology has gone digital. Models now work directly on sequence and structure, predicting how a protein folds and proposing designs that hold up at the bench, and capability in that half of the field is improving faster than any tool that came before it. Drug discovery is where the gains are most visible. Learned models are designing antibodies, de novo binders and small-molecule candidates that have reached clinical trials, and each generation of model has arrived stronger than the one before it.

The physical half is moving as well. Robotics, computer vision and machine vision are making the handling, imaging and judgment steps of an experiment cheaper and more repeatable each year, which turns work that once depended on a particular pair of hands into work a facility can run at volume.

Those two curves are now improving together, and biology is acquiring a scaling effect of a kind the field has not seen. Every one of those advances arrives at the same place: an instrument that moves material. Porcupine works there. We build the evaluation methods and the authorization standard that existing biosecurity frameworks call for but do not specify, so the record of a physical experiment stays intelligible to a person as the number of experiments grows.
